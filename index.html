Key takeaway

Conveyancing in Australia is the legal and administrative work behind buying, selling or transferring property. The practical sequence is to define the matter first, note whether a contract is signed, identify the state or territory, then ask each practitioner the same scoped questions about fees, disbursements, updates and settlement coordination. The Process In Plain Sequence

  1. Define the transaction: purchase, sale, transfer, off the plan purchase, commercial file, or a mixed matter.
  2. Fix the stage: before listing, before contract issue, after signing, during finance, or near settlement.
  3. Confirm the jurisdiction: the Australian Capital Territory, New South Wales, Northern Territory, Queensland, South Australia, Tasmania, Victoria or Western Australia.
  4. Prepare the facts: property address, parties, agent details, lender if any, contract status, and any family or ownership context for a transfer.
  5. Screen providers with identical questions: scope, documents reviewed, day to day contact, updates, professional fees and disbursements.
  6. Convert answers into a comparison: keep written notes so each practitioner is judged on the same file, not on confidence or profile polish.

What Changes For Each Matter Type

A residential purchase usually turns on contract review, finance and settlement timing. A residential sale often needs prompt contract preparation, discharge of any mortgage and coordination with the agent. First home buyer matters can require more explanation of process, checks and settlement steps. Off the plan buyers should ask how contract conditions and sunset or timing issues are explained before settlement is booked.

A transfer can involve different documents and ownership context, including family arrangements, while commercial conveyancing needs closer attention to contract terms and how the property is used for business. The useful habit is to brief every candidate with the same one paragraph file summary and ask what they would do next.

Questions That Test Scope Without Overclaiming

The first conversation is a screening step, not a commitment. The enquiry is described as free to submit and without obligation to proceed, which makes it sensible to compare scope before sharing documents. Ask whether the practitioner handles the exact transaction type, what information is needed before a useful fee explanation, and who the day to day contact will be.

  • Does the quoted scope include contract review, settlement coordination and routine correspondence?
  • Which costs are professional fees and which are disbursements such as searches or settlement charges?
  • What happens if the matter becomes more complex than expected?
  • What may fall outside the quote if contract terms change or settlement is delayed?

Jurisdiction, Documents And Red Flags

Compare providers against the state or territory where the property sits, not merely where the buyer lives. Conveyancing practice and process vary across jurisdictions, and local knowledge can affect document preparation, search requirements, settlement logistics and communication with agents and lenders.

Useful red flags are vague scope, reluctance to identify who does the work, fee talk before the file is described, and answers that treat a review count as proof of fit. A clear provider should state assumptions, exclusions, timing risks and what they need from you next. Keep notes from each call; the pattern across answers is usually more reliable than any single impressive sentence.

  1. Define the matter. Write one paragraph covering purchase, sale, transfer, off the plan or commercial context, contract status and jurisdiction.
  2. Standardise the questions. Ask every practitioner the same core questions about scope, documents, contact, updates, fees and disbursements.
  3. Record the answers. Keep written notes so comparisons are based on identical questions rather than memory.
  4. Confirm before proceeding. Contact the business directly to confirm scope, fees, availability and whether it can assist with the matter.

Common questions

Is an initial conveyancing enquiry a commitment? No. The process is described as free to submit and without obligation to proceed. Treat the first conversation as a screening step to learn whether the provider is appropriate before sharing documents or agreeing to paid work.

Should I choose a conveyancer from reviews alone? Reviews, star ratings and profile summaries are signals only. They do not replace direct answers about who will do the work, which documents are reviewed, how updates are given, and which costs are fees rather than disbursements.

Do conveyancing steps differ by state or territory? Yes. The relevant comparison is the state or territory where the property sits, not only where the buyer lives, because practice, documents, searches and settlement logistics can vary across jurisdictions.
